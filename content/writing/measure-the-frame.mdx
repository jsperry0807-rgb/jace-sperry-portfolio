Voxel engines attract premature cleverness. Greedy meshing, level of detail, indirect draws, bindless textures — every technique is interesting, and most of them are the wrong next step.

The engine I built before Valescent used a simple rule: **a milestone does not start until the previous one is measured.** Not "looks smooth on my machine". Measured, recorded, and written down.

## The numbers are part of the architecture

The slice I measured rendered 1 m blocks, 16³ meshlets, and 128³ chunk edges. The render ring held 256 chunks at 1080p, and the world generator produced a finite 1,024 km square with 18 biomes, rivers, lakes, caves, ores, landmarks, structures, and flora.

Those are not arbitrary constants. They are the *minimum profile* the M1 gate had to clear. Changing one is an architectural change, not a tuning knob.

- Smaller meshlets mean more draw calls and more bookkeeping.
- Larger chunks mean longer mesh builds and worse streaming granularity.
- A wider ring means more memory and more uploads per frame.

The gate exists because it is very easy to move all three at once and learn nothing.

## What actually gets recorded

A benchmark is only useful if it can be compared later. The repository keeps recorded runs, history, and fixtures next to the code, and the milestone status document links the evidence.

The measurements that mattered:

- **Frame time percentiles**, not just an average. A 60 fps average with 40 ms spikes feels worse than a stable 45.
- **Mesh build time per chunk**, because it competes with rendering for the same frame budget.
- **Memory per chunk and per meshlet**, since a streaming ring multiplies every allocation.
- **Upload stalls**, which are invisible in a clean demo and obvious when the camera moves.

The benchmark harness runs the same scenes so a change can be attributed. If a run cannot be reproduced, it does not count as evidence.

## What the measurements changed

Two decisions came directly out of numbers rather than taste.

First, the meshlet size stayed at 16³. Larger meshlets reduced draw calls but pushed build times past the point where streaming stayed smooth.

Second, streaming became a budgeted queue instead of "generate until it looks done". The engine now spends a fixed amount of work per frame, even if that means terrain arrives a moment later. A stable frame with late terrain is better than a complete world that hitches.

Neither of those is a novel technique. They are the kind of decisions you only make when you have the numbers in front of you.

## Honest limits

This is a solo project, not a shipped game. The measurements come from a small number of machines, GPU tests skip when no adapter is available, and the fixtures are not a representative benchmark suite for the hardware people actually own.

I try to say that in the status document instead of implying the engine is faster than it is. A measurement gate is not a marketing claim. It is a way to avoid lying to yourself between milestones.

<Note title="Reproduce it">
  The benchmark harness, fixtures, and recorded runs live in the repository next
  to the code they measure. If you want to check the numbers, start with
  `benchmarks/` rather than the README.
</Note>

## The takeaway

Optimization without a baseline is just reordering work. The gate costs a day per milestone and saves weeks of guessing later.

If you take one thing from this: write the number down before you write the optimization. The number is the specification.
